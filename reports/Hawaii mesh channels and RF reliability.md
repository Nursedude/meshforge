# Measure the commons before you join it

HawaiiMesh is a small, real, two-protocol community. The wiki lists ~130 Meshtastic and ~65 MeshCore nodes, and CoreScope counted 72 live MeshCore nodes on 2026-11-16. MeshForge can interoperate with it without depending on it by owning its own RF plane, brokers and RNS hub, and treating HawaiiMesh as an optional read-only leg plus an outbound observer contribution. All four HawaiiMesh Meshtastic channels (LongFast, HawaiiNet, hawaiimesh, alerts) use the public `AQ==` key on one LONG_FAST frequency. They share one RF channel, and **only "LongFast" counts as a "default channel" for the firmware's traffic floors**. Every relay in the default `ALL` mode floods all four channels whether it can decrypt them or not. Nobody has published Hawaii channel-utilization data, so how much load the extra channels add is UNKNOWN; the mechanism is VERIFIED in firmware source. The recent RF decline has no public corroboration. The best-fitting suspects are relay attrition after Hurricane Lala (2026-08-14), MQTT-dependent "inter-island" delivery, and a fleet split across presets and protocols. Atmosphere, humidity and the solar cycle cannot cause a step change. Each suspect leaves a different fingerprint at the two ends of a link. The EmComm claims on the live wiki are modest ("off-grid network for everyone", an `#alert` channel). They still clash with the wiki's own instruction to rely on internet MQTT for neighbor-island delivery, which is the network's core EmComm weakness. The tool ecosystem (CoreScope, MeshMonitor, PotatoMesh, CascadiaMesh's operating rules) already covers analytics and dashboards. MeshForge's defensible contribution is narrower: per-leg delivery truth, airtime accounting, and honest "cannot see" signals.

Evidence tiers follow the project's calibrated-claims rule. **VERIFIED** means checked this session with a quoted result. The live hawaiimesh.net and CoreScope reads (≈20:35–20:50Z 2026-11-16) and a hash/slot calculation run while writing this report count as VERIFIED. **BELIEVED** means reasoned or second-hand. **UNKNOWN** means not observable from here.

## Four AQ== channels share one slot, and only one of them is throttled

**Yes: all four HawaiiMesh channels draw on one shared airtime budget.** Every Meshtastic channel on a node uses the same modem settings, which "can **not** be unique per channel" ([channels.mdx](https://github.com/meshtastic/meshtastic/blob/0c02d499bdad36db9bd685532e3c8c9fc3d4c1a7/docs/configuration/radio/channels.mdx)). The receiver counts every frame it locks onto in ChUtil, including packets for other channels and every duplicate relay copy ([airtime.h](https://github.com/meshtastic/firmware/blob/68f47efddd10c4ed383adf90eace094e47779cac/src/airtime.h)). Splitting traffic into channels separates it cryptographically and in the app. It does not separate it on air. Running the firmware's hash and slot algorithm (script in the session scratchpad) reproduced the official US slots for LongFast (slot 20, 906.875 MHz) and MediumFast (slot 45, 913.125 MHz), which checks the implementation. It gives the four HawaiiMesh `AQ==` channels **distinct 8-bit hashes: LongFast 0x08, HawaiiNet 0x62, hawaiimesh 0x0e, alerts 0x1f**. VERIFIED (calculation). A node therefore decrypts only the channels it holds, but it hears and pays airtime for all four.

The 1-byte hash in the header is only a hint for decryption, so relaying does not respect channel boundaries. On 2.7.x, a packet a node cannot decrypt is still rebroadcast unless `rebroadcast_mode` is LOCAL_ONLY or KNOWN_ONLY ([RoutingModule.cpp v2.7.15](https://github.com/meshtastic/firmware/blob/v2.7.15.567b8ea/src/modules/RoutingModule.cpp)). From 2.8, `relayOpaquePacket()` relays such packets "only from the immutable outer routing header", with a dedup set added to prevent "an unbounded broadcast storm" ([NextHopRouter.cpp](https://github.com/meshtastic/firmware/blob/68f47efddd10c4ed383adf90eace094e47779cac/src/mesh/NextHopRouter.cpp)). Under the default `ALL` mode, **every CLIENT, ROUTER and ROUTER_LATE in Hawaiʻi floods the hawaiimesh, alerts and HawaiiNet traffic it hears, even with none of those channels configured.** Each extra channel therefore adds load in proportion to what it carries, multiplied by the existing relay fabric. It does not add a new relay structure. VERIFIED from source; not measured on air.

The important subtlety is throttling. The firmware treats a channel as "default" only when its key is `AQ==` **and** its name equals the preset name ([Channels.cpp](https://github.com/meshtastic/firmware/blob/68f47efddd10c4ed383adf90eace094e47779cac/src/mesh/Channels.cpp)). On LONG_FAST, only the channel named "LongFast" qualifies. HawaiiNet, hawaiimesh and alerts are **"public but not default"**. They inherit 2.8's privacy restrictions: a 15-bit position cap, and "Precise position no longer allowed on known-keys". They do **not** get the default-channel interval floors. In develop, the position floor applies only when position is sent on a default channel. The telemetry floor needs `hasDefaultChannel()`. In 2.7.15 the telemetry floor applied only if the *primary* channel was default ([NodeDB.cpp v2.7.15](https://github.com/meshtastic/firmware/blob/v2.7.15.567b8ea/src/mesh/NodeDB.cpp); [NodeDB.cpp develop](https://github.com/meshtastic/firmware/blob/68f47efddd10c4ed383adf90eace094e47779cac/src/mesh/NodeDB.cpp)). A node that makes HawaiiNet its primary channel, as the repo records for moc, moc1, moc4, kiai and moc5 ([review_provenance.md, 2026-10-13](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/audits/review_provenance.md)), therefore loses the default-channel floors. If its frequency slot is not pinned explicitly, it also leaves the shared frequency: **the same hash-and-slot calculation puts a HawaiiNet primary at slot 64, 917.875 MHz, a hawaiimesh primary at 927.375 MHz, and an alerts primary at 904.125 MHz**, all deaf to the LongFast mesh at 906.875 MHz. Whether those fleet boxes pin slot 20 is UNKNOWN from the records read (lehua's bring-up does pin "LONG_FAST slot 20"). The same trap catches any HawaiiMesh newcomer who follows the wiki's five-step guide but types the name into the primary slot instead of adding a secondary. Meshtastic's own October 2026 guidance warns against exactly this ([finding-your-local-mesh](https://github.com/meshtastic/meshtastic/blob/0c02d499bdad36db9bd685532e3c8c9fc3d4c1a7/blog/2026/October/finding-your-local-mesh.mdx)).

How much this matters comes down to arithmetic. On LONG_FAST a ~60-byte frame takes **≈682 ms**. A node reaches the firmware's 25% "polite" gate at about **1,320 transmissions per hour heard** (originals plus relays, all channels) and the 40% gate at about 2,100 per hour. These are computed figures, not measured. The polite gate silences only a node's own position, telemetry and neighbor-info. **Relays, text and bot replies are never gated** ([PositionModule.cpp and siblings](https://github.com/meshtastic/firmware/blob/68f47efddd10c4ed383adf90eace094e47779cac/src/modules/PositionModule.cpp)), so a saturated mesh degrades through collisions instead of backing off. The three secondary channels carry little chat; MeshCore's `#alert` shows 18 messages in total. Their real airtime costs are structural: two MeshCore↔Meshtastic bridges that inject foreign traffic, a weather bot that answers on air (the operator's own wx bot on HawaiiNet), and, on the LongFast row itself, MQTT downlink of neighbor-island traffic into local RF. The only local congestion measurement is the repo's: on 2026-10-03 the busiest fleet box logged **155 "Ch. util >25%. Skip send" lines in 3 h** ([radio_channel_load.py:5-7](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/src/utils/radio_channel_load.py)). That shows the knee is being crossed at one vantage point. It is not an island-wide series. The overall verdict on the AQ== channels: they **do** affect utilization, linearly with their traffic. The channels themselves are not the hazard. The hazards are automated injectors (bots, bridges, MQTT downlink) that the polite gate never throttles, and primaries renamed without pinning the slot. A preset change, as Wellington (LongFast ChUtil peaks above 65%, then reliable on ShortFast) and the Bay Area (MediumSlow) made, attacks per-packet airtime. A multi-channel plan adds packets. These are independent levers, and choosing between them belongs to the community ([why-your-mesh-should-switch-from-longfast](https://github.com/meshtastic/meshtastic/blob/0c02d499bdad36db9bd685532e3c8c9fc3d4c1a7/blog/2025/April/why-your-mesh-should-switch-from-longfast.mdx)).

## Own the RF plane, read HawaiiMesh as a leg, and contribute outbound

Two facts constrain decoupling. A node's secondary channels inherit the primary's preset and slot, so "private primary plus public secondary" separates keys but not RF. And one radio cannot sit on two slots. **The strongest pattern is therefore a separate RF plane**: the MOC's own private primary, custom key, preset and slot, as moc2/moc3 already run on SHORT_TURBO slot 8 ([SKILL.md:111-118](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/skills/meshforge/SKILL.md)), plus a *separate* radio for the HawaiiMesh LONG_FAST slot. On that radio HawaiiMesh becomes an observer leg. It runs CLIENT_MUTE (or a deliberate client role if the community wants the relay), keeps beacons minimal and `posPrec 0` as on lehua, and uplinks only to the box's **localhost** mosquitto with downlink off. That way HawaiiMesh never sees MeshForge traffic and MeshForge never needs HawaiiMesh's broker ([lehua_bringup_2026_08_29.md](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/plans/lehua_bringup_2026_08_29.md); [local_mqtt_architecture.md](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/research/local_mqtt_architecture.md)). Remote feeds become read-only enrichment with a tri-state status (ok / unobservable / error), never a liveness source. This includes `msh/US/HI/#` on the public broker and CoreScope's read API, which the repo already plans to read and not rebuild (CS-READ). If the feed goes dark, the MOC must say "cannot see HawaiiNet" rather than "HawaiiNet quiet" (honest-failure-modes #2). Inter-box traffic stays on the fleet's own RNS/LXMF fabric and local brokers, which ran through Lala without needing the WAN ([lala_outage_recovery_2026_08_27.md](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/research/lala_outage_recovery_2026_08_27.md)). BELIEVED as a design; parts are already fleet practice.

Under this pattern, an outage of hawaiimesh.net costs MeshForge only the CoreScope view, the channel-plan reference page and any remote feed. RF reception of HawaiiMesh, the MOC's own slot, local brokers and RNS routing all keep working. The coupling that remains today is in the fleet. kiai uplinks HawaiiNet to the public `mqtt.meshtastic.org` under `msh/US/HI`, and moc4 uplinks to a third-party broker ([review_provenance.md](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/audits/review_provenance.md)). Both need a "broker unreachable" witness. Both also raise a consent question MeshForge has not modelled: a grep found no `ok_to_mqtt` handling anywhere in `src/` or `.claude/`. On the MeshCore side the fleet already *contributes* rather than consumes: **"meshanchor R1" relayed 50 packets in 24 h** from the ITO (Hawaiʻi Island) region, first seen 2026-09-01 ([CoreScope /api/nodes](https://corescope.hawaiimesh.net/api/nodes)). That makes the fleet part of the commons, with the duty of care that brings: R1 should follow whatever advert and scoping norms the community adopts.

R1's two alarming-looking labels are both benign. **`foreign: true` is a sticky, historical ingest flag.** CoreScope's own frontend defines it as "An advert from this node was flagged outside the configured area at ingestion. This historical flag does not indicate its current location." Every foreign-flagged node on the instance was first seen between 2026-08-29 and 2026-09-27, the instance's first month, and no node first seen later carries the flag. VERIFIED (served JS plus `/api/nodes`, first page of 50). The cause is BELIEVED to be a geofence change after early ingest; it is not a fault in R1. **Grade D (usefulness score 0.251) measures traffic share and graph centrality as seen by eight observers, not RF quality.** R1's axes are traffic share 1.4% ("Redundant" band), bridge 0.030 ("Marginal"), coverage 0.657 and redundancy 0.26. D is the **modal grade** on the instance (≈A1, B2, C12, D24, F12). The letter cutoffs are server-side and UNKNOWN. The separate "meshanchor rs1" room server shows grade F with 0 relays, which is what a node that does not forward would score (BELIEVED). The operator says rs1 = R1; CoreScope keys them as two nodes with two roles. Practical reading: a D-graded repeater at a site the observers see as redundant is doing exactly what a leaf repeater should. A better grade would take an observer near Hilo or a site that actually bridges, not a configuration change.

## Relay attrition and MQTT dependence outrank the sky as decline suspects

**No public source documents a Hawaiʻi decline, and none refutes one.** The decline is the operator's observation. Ranking its causes rests on mechanism and timing, and only the operator's measurements can confirm one. Physics rules out several popular explanations. At 915 MHz, gaseous absorption is ~0.005 dB/km, a humid-versus-dry swing over 200 km is ~0.1 dB, rain loss is under 0.001 dB/km, and vog particles are far smaller than a 33 cm wavelength. Solar cycle 25 matters even less: F2-layer critical frequencies of 10–15 MHz are ~60× below the band, and ground paths never reach the ionosphere. These coefficients come from ITU-R recommendations recalled from memory (BELIEVED). Trade-wind-inversion frequency over Hawaiʻi has risen ~5–6% per decade since 1979 ([Longman/Giambelluca](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/e1c8fbb4-3a45-43f1-a9cd-9c9f8e91d6c5)), and ducting near Hawaiʻi is well documented: elevated ducts ~110 m thick, evaporation ducts ~15 m ([Winning et al. 2024](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/17/6851/2024/amt-17-6851-2024.pdf); [Alappattu et al.](https://calhoun.nps.edu/entities/publication/780fceae-cab4-4cc7-a8e1-555190173aa3)). That produces fade *and* enhancement on hour-to-day timescales, which has the wrong time-shape for a 2025–26 step. Geometry carries a sharper lesson. Two 10 m coastal nodes have a ~26 km radio horizon, so **beach-to-beach links across the Kaʻiwi (~42 km) or ʻAlenuihāhā (~48 km) channels have no line of sight and work only under super-refraction**, while high site to high site (Kaʻala to Haleakalā) keeps 20–40 dB of LONG_FAST margin. "It used to work" can simply describe the best ducting days. PHYSICS, from the formulas in the notes.

The network-side and infrastructure causes fit the "lately" timing better. The fleet's own record holds a dated step. In **Hurricane Lala (2026-08-14) the grid was down about a week and the network degraded for about two**. The regional relay Farley-server (STATION_G2, ROUTER_LATE, 8 dBi at 23 ft, reaching Mauna Kea, Mauna Loa and HPP), "part of Hawaii Meshtastic critical infrastructure", went down and reports no battery telemetry, "so its loss is only visible as silence". Whether it, or the redundant FOh-ENV, came back is not recorded ([field_ecomm_and_dutycycle_fleet.md:692-711](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/plans/field_ecomm_and_dutycycle_fleet.md)). With hop limit 3, losing an elevated relay does not thin the mesh gracefully: "it partitions". The second suspect is the wiki's own design. If neighbor-island delivery always rode MQTT, any change in a bridge operator's node, internet link or broker would read as "inter-island RF got worse" with zero RF change. The third is fragmentation. Some of the mesh's operators now run MeshCore (72 nodes, most first seen since late August); the fleet itself split moc2/moc3 onto SHORT_TURBO; 2.8-alpha screen setups land on LongTurbo; and any unpinned renamed primary moves to another slot. Each of these removes relays from the LONG_FAST mesh without touching path loss. Two further mechanisms are real but unconfirmed for Hawaiʻi. Firmware 2.7.20 exempted ROUTER_LATE from congestion scaling ([v2.7.20 notes](https://newreleases.io/project/github/meshtastic/firmware/release/v2.7.20.6658ec2)), and a ChUtil step at an upgrade date would show it. Hawaiian Electric's Landis+Gyr Gridstream rollout runs on Wi-SUN networks ([Landis+Gyr](https://landisgyr.com/news/hawaiian-utility-selects-landisgyrs-gridstream-technology-for-smart-grid-deployment)); Wi-SUN's use of 902–928 MHz is BELIEVED, and the deployment timing is UNKNOWN. That would raise the urban noise floor and eat weak links first. No FCC action on NextNav's Lower 900 MHz proposal had produced an order in the sources found ([Broadband Breakfast](https://broadbandbreakfast.com/nextnav-cleared-for-more-coexistence-tests/)).

One caution comes from the fleet's own history. Part of what the record calls "interference" was produced by the instrument. Heltec v3 RNodes generate a steady −71 dBm reading themselves, and interference-avoidance then holds TX (2026-10-28 bench: 9/20 delivered with IA on, 20/20 with it off) ([rnode_v3_ia_hold_offer_back_2026_10_28.md](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/research/rnode_v3_ia_hold_offer_back_2026_10_28.md)). Any noise-floor evidence has to come from an instrument that has been validated first. The table below ranks the suspects by the fingerprint each leaves. The discriminators work only when **measured at both ends** of a link.

| Rank | Suspect | Confirms it | Refutes it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Relay attrition (Lala, solar/battery, corrosion) | Named relay IDs silent since a date; battery trending down; pre-dawn dropouts; failures only on paths through that relay | All relays heard at baseline SNR |

A second blind spot affects trend-reading. The CoreScope instance is about 2.5 months old, and observers have been added on 10-09, 10-31, 11-08 and 11-10. **Packet counts on it will rise because observer coverage grew, even if the mesh shrank.** Its data is a baseline that starts after Lala, not a before-and-after comparison. The stale Waikoloa Solar observer, last heard at about 06:45 HST, fits the solar-attrition fingerprint, but one sample proves nothing (SPECULATION).

## The tagline says off-grid, but inter-island delivery runs over the internet

The live wiki makes fewer EmComm claims than the earlier search-snippet research suggested. Across eight fetched pages, the only resilience language is the meta description "Hawaii's off-grid network for everyone", the MeshCore `#alert` row "emergency alerts (Delta-EDC)", and the MQTT note. The words "EmComm", "disaster", "resilien*" and "disclaimer" do not appear. The only hedge is a footnote: "Accuracy is not guaranteed, implied, or, in some cases, attempted" ([home](https://hawaiimesh.net/landing-page/home)). VERIFIED. The stronger phrase "a secure, off grid, emergency communication system" came from a search-engine extract and does not appear on any page fetched live. It is UNKNOWN whether it ever existed or sits on an unlisted page; quote nothing from it.

Even the modest claim fails one specific test. **The same wiki that says "off-grid" tells users to depend on internet MQTT for neighbor-island delivery.** Geometry explains why: low channel crossings have no line of sight. That MQTT path disappears exactly when the internet does, and the wiki names no Meshtastic broker, topic root or bridge operator, so nobody can audit the dependency ([Meshtastic Radio Setting](https://hawaiimesh.net/networks/meshtastic/meshtastic-radio-setting)). Four other weaknesses add to it. `AQ==` is a published key, so nothing on these channels is private, and 2.8 removes precise position on known-key channels, which matters for any search-and-rescue use. The alert and bridge paths each depend on one person (pmow, WH6EPS), with no documented failover, rate limit or loop rule. A live `#emergency` channel exists on MeshCore that the wiki does not mention, which splits where people would look in a crisis. And the network has a **recorded outage**: Lala left it degraded for about two weeks.

The wider evidence base supports "people used it and some messages got through", not measured reliability. In western North Carolina after Helene, a secondary guide reports the mesh "briefly became unusable when newcomers spammed status updates every 30 seconds" ([Pamfinds](https://pamfinds.com/guides/disaster-comms-meshtastic-meshcore/)). In Lahaina the surviving repeater was "overwhelmed" while cellular service failed ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2024/04/17/1245396324/maui-fire-report-communications-breakdown)). A shared LoRa channel faces the same surge dynamics at far lower capacity. No evidence connects HI-EMA, county EMAs or Hawaii ARES to Meshtastic. ARES's documented digital direction is Winlink and AREDN ([old.hawaiiares.net](https://old.hawaiiares.net/)), and federal PACE guidance asks that auxiliary systems be documented and **practiced** ([CISA PACE](https://cybersecurity.gov/sites/default/files/2023-05/23_0426_ncswic_PACE-Plan_508.pdf)). Verdict: as a volunteer community's supplemental mesh, HawaiiMesh's framing is **reasonable and not an overclaim**. It would become one if presented as dependable when the grid or internet is down before it has dated drills, measured delivery (including a surge test), published power autonomy per relay, disclosure of its internet dependencies, and a written role with a served agency. MeshForge's own public standard is the template. Its strongest claim stays at "one 24 h serial-to-serial lab run: 1,436 of 1,436 broadcasts crossed", and it never says "delivered" for an unconfirmed DM ([landscape_2026_11_09.md:261-263](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/research/landscape_2026_11_09.md)).

## Cascadia, CoreScope and the observer tools already cover analytics

"CascadiaNet" almost certainly means **CascadiaMesh**, a community **MeshCore** network across British Columbia, Western Washington and Oregon, with ~2,900–3,900 live nodes on its JavaScript counter ([CascadiaMesh](https://cascadiamesh.org/)). The mapping is BELIEVED; no separate "CascadiaNet" was found. It keeps a mesh that size usable through policy, not dashboards. The rules are flood adverts no more often than every 70 h (live-verified by the operator's 10-31 study, which outranks the 48 h figure in search snippets), per-city region tag chains, firmware ≥1.15, and a role-based web flasher with rollback that runs its MediaMesh "radio2" fork. MediaMesh puts photos and voice on a second profile and refuses community channels ([Regions](https://cascadiamesh.org/regions/); [MediaMesh Flasher](https://cascadiamesh.org/mediamesh-flasher/)). The transferable lesson for Hawaiʻi is to **cap advert airtime by policy, scope floods by geography, and audit compliance**. Those rules arrived because MeshCore developed congestion of its own ([MeshCore #1223](https://gh.hhj520.top/meshcore-dev/MeshCore/issues/1223); [region filtering](https://blog.meshcore.io/2026/01/20/region-filtering)). Moving from Meshtastic to MeshCore moves the congestion problem rather than removing it.

**CoreScope** (GPL-3.0, 211★, 3,162 commits as fetched 2026-11-16) is the MeshCore analyzer HawaiiMesh already runs. Observer radios publish raw packets to `meshcore/{IATA}/{PUBKEY}/packets`. It traces packets across observers, monitors observer health, keeps a hash-collision matrix and runs a scope audit ([Kpa-clawbot/CoreScope](https://github.com/Kpa-clawbot/CoreScope)). Its README names no channel-utilization or airtime metric. On the Meshtastic side, **MeshMonitor** (BSD-3, 731★) is the closest thing to a multi-protocol MOC, and it ships an airtime cutoff that **pauses bot traffic when the mesh is busy**. **PotatoMesh** (Apache-2.0, 558★) federates instances by crawling peers, with no broker at all. **Meshview** and **Malla** are MQTT-fed analyzers with top-talker views ([MeshMonitor](https://github.com/Yeraze/meshmonitor); [PotatoMesh](https://github.com/l5yth/potato-mesh); [Meshview](https://github.com/pablorevilla-meshtastic/meshview); [Malla](https://github.com/zenitraM/malla)). The repo's own 11-04/11-09 assessment stands: MeshForge does not beat CoreScope at MeshCore analytics, MeshMonitor at dashboards, or mesh-api/AMMB at MT↔MC relay ([landscape_2026_11_04.md](https://github.com/Nursedude/meshforge/blob/main/.claude/research/landscape_2026_11_04.md)). Two gaps remain open in this survey. No tool combines per-node self-reported `channel_utilization` and `air_util_tx`, observed packet counts by originator and relayer, and computed time-on-air into an **airtime-offender ranking** labelled with how much of the mesh it could observe. And no tool joins meshes that cannot hear each other while reporting delivery truth per leg. Both fit MeshForge's charter. Neither requires MeshForge to become the community's platform.

## Conclusion

The question has narrowed. In October it was about the community's presets and claims. The live data makes it about evidence nobody has collected yet. HawaiiMesh's channel plan is not reckless: `AQ==` secondaries cost airtime in proportion to their traffic, and they cost nothing in partitioning as long as no one renames a primary. Its EmComm framing is modest. What neither the wiki nor the operator can currently answer is whether the inter-island links that "used to work" were RF at all, which relays Lala took, and how much of the shared slot the bots, bridges and MQTT downlinks consume. These questions can be measured. A two-ended census of relays, `via_mqtt` share, neighbor counts and ChUtil answers them better than any change of preset, protocol or platform.

The fleet is no longer an outside observer. It runs a HawaiiMesh MeshCore repeater, the HawaiiNet weather bot and two public uplinks, so the first honest step is to audit its own footprint (slot pinning, bot budget, broker witnesses) before offering anything to the community. That also sets the role in which MeshForge contributes most. Measurement tools already exist in plenty. What is missing is an instrument that reports what share of a commons each participant uses and says plainly when it cannot see, and MeshForge can be that instrument, starting with its own nodes.
